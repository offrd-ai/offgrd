# OFFGRD — 2:22 Companion Script
**Companion to the 60s spot.** 382 words · projected **2:22** at your measured read. Same open, same close, same voice — so the two run as a pair.
**Audience:** high school head coach · offensive or defensive coordinator who'd actually hold the tablet.

---

## 1. What this one does differently

The 60s spot sells the idea. This one earns the trial. It walks O Caller and D Caller feature by feature — the section 4 inventory that 160 words couldn't carry — and it spends its longest stretch on Friday night, because that's the part a coach can picture himself doing.

Three rules I held to:

**The honesty beat stays in.** The THIN badge, the red unresolved tag, "never draws a rusher without a charted stunt" — no competitor says *here's what I don't know*, and a coach who has been burned by a confident-and-wrong scout report will hear it. It's in Block 6.

**Offline stays scoped to the callers.** O Caller and D Caller work offline. The rest of the app doesn't, and the script never implies it does. That's the claim that survives a coach testing it in a dead-wifi press box.

**The caller is not hands-free, and the script says so.** An earlier draft of Block 3 claimed it tracked the drive "without you touching it." That was wrong — punt, downs, takeaway and score are buttons the coach taps. The corrected block names the input plainly and claims only what's true: you log the play, it carries the state forward, and you never re-enter what it already knows. That's a better promise anyway, because it survives a coach using it.

**No scheme names on opponent data.** Their formations and attacks are described structurally, the way your chart actually holds them. Your own calls get their real names, because those are yours.

## 2. VO SCRIPT — eight blocks

Same spelling rule: **`OFF-GRID`**, never OFFGRD. Same settings — male 35–55 American low-mid, Multilingual v2, stability 50–60, similarity 75, style 0–15, speed 0.95.

### Block 1 — cold open *(identical to the 60s spot)*
```
You already know what they do on third and long.

It's in the film. It's just spread across a week of clips, a legal pad, and somebody's spreadsheet.

By the time it's a call sheet, half your week is gone.

OFF-GRID pulls it into one place.
```

### Block 2 — chart it once
```
Chart your film once.

OFF-GRID reads your breakdown the way you exported it. No re-tagging, no converting, no learning somebody else's names for your own formations. Map your vocabulary one time and it holds everywhere after.

Scout cards come out the other side, already built.
```

### Block 3 — Friday night, the situation
```
Friday night, you open O Caller.

You log the play — the call, the result, and how the drive ended. Punt, downs, takeaway, score. That's the input. Everything else it carries forward: down, distance, hash, field zone, series, quarter, possession. You never re-enter what it already knows.

The only thing on your screen is the call.
```

### Block 4 — what they're about to give you
```
Up top is what to expect. Their most likely front, their most likely coverage, how often they pressure — and the number of charted snaps behind all three, so you know how much to trust it.

Underneath, your own calls. Not a library. Your book, ranked for this exact situation, with what each one has done against this look.
```

### Block 5 — one tap
```
You make the call. One tap grades it.

That tap logs the play, updates your self-scout, sharpens the next prediction, and writes your call sheet while you stand there.

If the spotter got a down wrong, fix it in the log and everything downstream fixes itself.
```

### Block 6 — it tells you what it doesn't know
```
And when it isn't sure, it says so.

Small sample gets flagged. A formation it can't resolve stays in your words and stays marked. It will not draw a rusher you never charted. Every count reconciles, and it shows you the receipt.

Nobody gets beat by the play they were told was a sixty percent tendency off four snaps.
```

### Block 7 — the other side, and the booth
```
D Caller runs the same way against their offense. Their structure, their tendencies, your fronts and coverages, ranked the same way.

There's a booth mode for the box. A guided mode for a first-year coordinator, and one with the hand-holding turned off.

By Saturday morning your self-scout is written — call family, snaps, yards per play, efficiency. Nothing to type up.
```

### Block 8 — the close *(identical to the 60s spot)*
```
And when the press box wifi drops, both of them keep working.

OFF-GRID. Never off guard.
```

## 4. Why I'm not giving you timings yet

On the 60s spot I estimated 76 seconds from word count and your actual read came in at 60. That's a 21% miss, and it meant retiming every beat after the fact. I'm not repeating it.

This time the estimate is built from *your measured rate* — 160 words over 56.6 s of speech in the 60s render, or 2.83 words per second. At 382 words that's about 135 s of speech plus roughly 8 s of beats between blocks: **2:22**.

So: **render these eight blocks, send me the MP3s, and I'll build the animatic against the real durations** — same method as the 60s, cutting visuals against continuous blocks so no line ever clips at a scene boundary. If it comes back long, blocks 3 and 7 are the ones with slack.

If a block comes back sounding rushed, the two most likely are 4 and 5 — they carry the most information per second. Those are also the easiest to split further.

Blocks 1 and 8 are word-for-word identical to the 60s spot.
